Two models, one race, different answers

The tracker shows the methods agreeing or disagreeing about the country. This page takes them down to individual races, where disagreement stops being an average and starts being a claim about a specific seat that one of the models is getting wrong. That is the useful question: not which forecast is best, but what does this one know that the other one doesn't.

{{- with $c }}

Our polling model is deliberately ignorant. It knows the national tide and how each state has voted, and nothing else — not who is running, not who is retiring, not how much money anyone has raised. Set against a professional forecast that knows all of those things, every place the two diverge is a place where candidates, incumbency or local conditions are doing the work. Reading the gap is reading how much elections are about something other than the partisanship of the state.

How to read a disagreement

Three things can produce a gap between two forecasts of the same seat, and telling them apart is the exercise.

{{- with $top }}

Something one model knows and the other cannot

{{ .state }} is the widest disagreement among competitive seats this week. Our model has it at {{ if gt .ours 0.0 }}D+{{ printf "%.1f" .ours }}{{ else }}R+{{ printf "%.1f" (mul .ours -1) }}{{ end }}; the professional forecast has it at {{ if gt .gw 0.0 }}D+{{ printf "%.1f" .gw }}{{ else }}R+{{ printf "%.1f" (mul .gw -1) }}{{ end }}, a gap of {{ printf "%.1f" (math.Abs .gap) }} points. {{- with .pres_2024 }} The state's 2024 presidential margin was {{ if gt . 0.0 }}D+{{ printf "%.1f" . }}{{ else }}R+{{ printf "%.1f" (mul . -1) }}{{ end }}, which is where our model starts and very nearly where it finishes.{{ end }} Our model is reading the state. The professional forecast is reading the ballot. When the gap runs in the direction of a long-serving incumbent of the state's minority party, incumbency is usually the whole story — and it is a real effect that our model has no way to see.

A model running out of road

Not every gap is about a race. Across the seats that are not close, our margins run {{- with $c.stats.mean_signed_gap_safe }} {{ printf "%.1f" (math.Abs .) }} points more {{ if lt . 0.0 }}Republican{{ else }}Democratic{{ end }}{{ end }} than the professional forecast on average — not because we disagree about Wyoming, but because turning partisan lean into a margin with a straight line overshoots at the extremes, where there are not enough voters left to swing. That is a fault in our machinery rather than a claim about any seat, and it is the reason the table below sorts competitive races to the top.

The archive behind all of this

Every forecaster we are permitted to collect, captured once a day and written to disk as raw bytes before anything is parsed. That ordering is the whole design: when a source changes its layout in October and a parser quietly starts returning nonsense, the stored bytes let us fix the parser and reprocess the entire history. If only parsed output were kept, that history would be gone.

Alongside the bytes sits a manifest of SHA-256 hashes, committed on the day each capture was taken. It is a commitment without a disclosure: when the archive is released after the election, anyone can check that the released bytes are bit-identical to what was collected on the dates claimed. The full archive, including the forecasters who cannot be shown here during the cycle, becomes a public data resource once the election is over.
