What goes into each number

Four categories, each a family of forecasts that share a way of knowing. Our own two models get the most detail because they are the ones you can hold us to.

CategoryWhat its members have in common
Fundamentals Predicts from conditions rather than opinion: the economy, presidential approval, seats defended. Knows nothing about candidates or polls.
Polling Starts from what people tell pollsters, then averages, weights, or corrects for how early it is.
Professional Published statistical forecasts combining most of the above plus candidate quality, fundraising and local knowledge.
Markets Prices from people betting money — an aggregate of everyone else's models plus whatever the traders think they know.

Expert ratings are collected but kept out of all four averages: “Lean R” is not a number, and choosing which number to make it is a modelling decision. They have their own section and a graphic.

Ray Fair's vote-share equations

Three equations estimated together — presidential, on-term House and midterm House — on data back to 1916. Reads the economy directly from growth and inflation where ours reads it through approval, and carries an explicit balancing term, so a party that has done well is predicted to lose ground at the following midterm. Where the polling comes from

The model does not run its own polling average; it takes one, which makes the choice of whose a modelling decision.

SourceWhat it is
Silver Bulletin Generic-ballot average with house-effect adjustments and recency weighting. The series this model reads.
RealClearPolling Simpler unweighted average of recent polls, collected as a second opinion.
Race to the WH Per-race poll list, collected for the Senate.
Wikipedia's aggregator table A CC BY-SA table listing several aggregators' current generic-ballot averages side by side — Decision Desk HQ, RealClearPolling, Silver Bulletin, Race to the WH and others. Each row is attributed to the aggregator that produced it and inherits that aggregator's own licence, so nothing is republished that its owner has not permitted. This is what gives the polling category enough contributors to clear the disclosure floor, and it is the reason a polling average appears at all.

The named three permit collection but not per-poll republication during the cycle. Note what the polling category average actually mixes: most aggregators publish TODAY's generic ballot, while our own model contributes its ELECTION-DAY tide, which is that number shrunk toward the eventual result. They are both polling-based estimates of the national House margin, but they are not estimates of the same instant.

Markets

Not models. A price is what someone will pay for a contract that pays out if an event happens; reading it as a probability is a good approximation rather than an identity — fees, the cost of tying up capital until November, and a documented tendency to overprice longshots all push it around at the extremes.

A price here is the LAST TRADE, which on a thin market can be hours old and on either side of a wide spread. Nothing smooths it, and no market on this page is adjusted for the longshot bias its own literature documents.

Markets price control, not 50 seats. Democratic Senate control needs 51, because a 50–50 tie is broken by a Republican vice-president. Everywhere this site compares against a market it uses our 51-seat figure{{ with $p }}{{ with .prob_D_51_plus }} ({{ printf "%.0f%%" (mul . 100) }}), not the 50-plus one ({{ printf "%.0f%%" (mul $p.prob_D_50_plus 100) }}){{ end }}{{ end }}.

No exchange lists a seat-count market for 2026 that resolves to a single number — PredictIt runs one in buckets ("192 or fewer"), which is not a seat count and is deliberately not parsed as one — so the markets series is absent from the seat-count view on the tracker.

What is not published, and why

Sources that permit collection but not republication

Those appear only inside an average with at least three gated contributors, so no individual forecast can be backed out by subtraction. The floor counts gated sources rather than all sources: a source we publish by name adds nothing to anyone's protection, since a reader can subtract it straight back out. Enforced in code, and the aggregator re-derives it from its own output and refuses to write if it is violated. {{- if gt $d.suppressed_cells 0 }} Today it withholds {{ $d.suppressed_cells }} cells.{{ end }}

Licensed indices, and why there is no House table

District forecasts are built from a licensed partisan index. Given the national tide, which we publish, that index is exactly recoverable from any district forecast:

PVI = (district margin − national tide) ÷ 2

So no district margins are published. Chamber totals are: a seat count is a sum over 435 districts and cannot be inverted back to any of them. The Senate has no such problem — its state lean is our own reconstruction from public-domain returns.

What is published

Category averages, our own models in full, the individual forecasts whose licences permit it, and a manifest of SHA-256 hashes for every capture, committed on the day it was taken. When the full archive is released after the election, anyone can verify the released bytes are bit-identical to what was collected on the dates claimed.
