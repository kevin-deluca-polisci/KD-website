What goes into each number

Five families, each a group of forecasts that share a way of knowing. They are listed here in the order the site draws them, which runs from least modelled to most.

FamilyWhat its members have in common
Polling Starts from what people tell pollsters, then averages, weights, or corrects for how early it is.
Markets Prices from people betting money — an aggregate of everyone else's models plus whatever the traders think they know.
Fundamentals Predicts from conditions rather than opinion: the economy, presidential approval, seats defended. Knows nothing about candidates or polls.
Professional Published statistical forecasts combining most of the above plus candidate quality, fundraising and local knowledge. Currently draws no line on the chart — everything in this family is either licence-restricted or, in one case, withheld on the evidence below. What happened.
Academic Specifications published in the political-science forecasting literature, rebuilt here and run on our data. Four of them, disagreeing more with each other than any other family does.
{{/* The one thing about this table that is genuinely confusing, said rather than left for the reader to work out. */}}

A forecast can belong to more than one family, and several do. The first four labels describe a METHOD — what the forecast looks at. “Academic” describes PROVENANCE — where it was published. Those are different questions, so a referendum model published in a journal is an academic forecast and a fundamentals one, and it counts in both averages.

Expert ratings are collected but kept out of every average: “Lean R” is not a number, and choosing which number to make it is a modelling decision. They have their own section and a graphic.

Ray Fair's vote-share equations

Three equations estimated together — presidential, on-term House and midterm House — on data back to 1916. Reads the economy directly from growth and inflation where ours reads it through approval, and carries an explicit balancing term, so a party that has done well is predicted to lose ground at the following midterm. Professional forecasts

Published models combining polling, fundamentals, candidate quality and money. The category we are least free to reproduce.

Why this family shows nothing today. Every other forecaster here is collected under a licence that permits an average but not a per-forecaster figure, and a category average needs at least three such contributors before it can be shown. That left one openly licensed model — Grant Williams' — carrying the family on its own, and on 2026-08-22 we stopped publishing it. Not for a licence reason. His seat numbers carry a units error, and this archive should not publish a number it can show to be wrong.

His model builds each district's margin from its Cook PVI. Regressing his own published district margins on the PVI he ships beside them recovers that step exactly:

ChamberFitted relationshipR²
House (435 districts)margin = 5.69 + 1.114 × PVI0.978
Senate (35 races)margin = 6.14 + 1.199 × PVI0.949

An R² of 0.978 across 435 districts means that line is the district model; polling enters only eight of them. Cook PVI is a share deviation: a district three points more Democratic in share is six points more Democratic in margin, so the unit-consistent multiplier is 2.0. Our own seat model uses exactly 2.0, and his state PVIs agree with ours in scale, so this is one multiplier on one index rather than two different indices.

A coefficient of 1.11 compresses every district 45% toward the national environment, which in a D+5.7 year turns a large block of Republican-leaning seats into Democratic-favoured ones. Re-running his districts with his own uncertainty and only that number changed:

PVI multiplierExpected D seats
1.114 — his256.5
1.7 — an empirical House estimate233.3
2.0 — unit-consistent226.9

His published figure is 250.79. The 226.9 is where the markets, the polling family and our own curve all sit, so the entire gap between him and everything else on this page was that one coefficient. What is not affected is his national margin, which comes from a separate model, and nothing here is a claim that the rest of his work is unsound: his file is internally consistent to the decimal, which is the only reason the error was diagnosable from outside at all.

Collection continues daily and every row is kept. His outputs are gitignored and overwritten on each run, so this archive is the only record that his forecast existed on any given day — which is worth more when a model turns out to have a fault in it, not less. The full series will be in the post-election release.

Reading a ladder

Some of these contracts are not a price but a ladder: a row of markets, each paying out if the final number lands in one bucket. Kalshi runs one over Democratic seat counts (“218–221”, “Above 249”), and Kalshi and Polymarket both run one over the national House popular-vote margin (“Democrats, 8 to 10%”). A ladder is a probability distribution rather than a single number, which is more than any other source on this site provides, and it is where the market seat count and the market vote margin come from. Without it the markets column would hold chamber-control probabilities and nothing else — a control price does not imply a seat count, and it certainly does not imply a vote share.

Two conventions turn a ladder into a number, and both are choices rather than arithmetic. Prices are bid/ask midpoints across a dozen contracts, each carrying its own spread, so they do not sum to one; everything is normalised by their sum rather than trusted as a distribution. And an open-ended bucket has no honest representative value — “Above 249” is a claim about everything up to 435 — so it is read as one more bucket of its neighbours’ width. That understates the tails on purpose. The alternative, using the chamber maximum, would have “Above 249” imply an average of 342 seats.

Kalshi’s Republican bucket is coarse, and this is what it costs. On the margin ladder Kalshi resolves the Democratic side in two-point steps but collapses every Republican outcome into a single “Republicans win” contract — one bucket holding about 9% of the mass and spanning the entire other half of the line. A value has to be assumed for it. On the 2026-08-21 book:
Assume that bucket sits atImplied margin
R+1 — one neighbour’s width, what we useD+7.83
R+2D+7.74
R+3D+7.65
R+5D+7.47
R+8 — a Republican waveD+7.19

Polymarket resolves both tails — Republicans 0–2, 2–4, 4–6 and 6%+ — so it needs no such assumption, and on the same day it read D+7.58. That lands between the R+2 and R+3 rows, which says the convention overstates the Democratic margin by roughly a quarter of a point. Both exchanges are collected partly so that this can be checked rather than asserted: the better-resolved market is a standing measurement of how much the coarser one’s missing tail is worth.

PredictIt also runs a seat-count market in buckets (“192 or fewer”), but its ladder is too coarse to read as a distribution and is deliberately not parsed as one. Its contribution here is chamber and per-race probabilities.

What is not published, and why

Sources that permit collection but not republication

Those appear only inside an average with at least three gated contributors, so no individual forecast can be backed out by subtraction. The floor counts gated sources rather than all sources: a source we publish by name adds nothing to anyone's protection, since a reader can subtract it straight back out. Enforced in code, and the aggregator re-derives it from its own output and refuses to write if it is violated. {{- if gt $d.suppressed_cells 0 }} Today it withholds {{ $d.suppressed_cells }} cells.{{ end }}

Licensed indices, and why there is no House table

District forecasts are built from a licensed partisan index. Given the national tide, which we publish, that index is exactly recoverable from any district forecast:

PVI = (district margin − national tide) ÷ 2

So no district margins are published. Chamber totals are: a seat count is a sum over 435 districts and cannot be inverted back to any of them. The Senate has no such problem — its state lean is our own reconstruction from public-domain returns.

What is published

Category averages, our own models in full, the individual forecasts whose licences permit it, and a manifest of SHA-256 hashes for every capture, committed on the day it was taken. When the full archive is released after the election, anyone can verify the released bytes are bit-identical to what was collected on the dates claimed.
