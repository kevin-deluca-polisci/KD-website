What goes into each number

The tracker shows four averages. Each one is a family of forecasts that share a way of knowing, and the interesting thing about the chart is that the four families disagree — so it matters what is inside each. This page lists every model that contributes, what it is made of, and where to go and read it. Our own two models get the most detail, because they are the ones you can hold us to.

Ray Fair's vote-share equations

The oldest continuously published fundamentals model in the field, run at Yale since the 1970s. Fair estimates three equations at once — the presidential vote, the on-term House vote and the midterm House vote — on data going back to 1916, and the headline finding is that the economy moves all three in much the same way. Two features make it a useful contrast with our model. It reads the economy directly, from growth and inflation, where we read it through presidential approval; and it carries an explicit balancing term, so a party that has done well is predicted to lose ground at the following midterm rather than to keep it. Fair also finds no presidential coattails on same-term House races: presidential and House votes move together because the economy moves both, not because one drags the other.

Inputs that are not forecasts

Two sources feed the fundamentals family without predicting anything themselves, and they are listed here so that “our own data” never has to be taken on trust. FRED supplies real disposable personal income per capita, pulled fresh every day. The MIT Election Data and Science Lab supplies certified official returns under a public-domain dedication, which is what our state partisan lean is reconstructed from. That reconstruction is why the Senate table can be published at all — see what is not published.

Where the polling comes from

Our model does not run its own polling average. It takes one from someone who does, which makes the choice of whose average to use a modelling decision in its own right.

Silver Bulletin publishes a generic-congressional-ballot average with house-effect adjustments and a recency weighting, and is the series this model currently reads. RealClearPolling publishes a simpler unweighted average of recent polls, collected here as a second opinion — the gap between the two is a decent measure of how much the weighting choices matter. Race to the WH maintains a per-race poll list which we collect for the Senate.

All three permit collection but not per-poll republication during the cycle. Their numbers reach this site only inside a category average, never as individual rows. That is a licence constraint, not a judgment about their quality — and we are asking.

Markets

Not models. A market price is what someone will pay for a contract that pays out if an event happens, and reading that price as a probability is a good approximation rather than an identity — fees, the cost of tying up capital until November, and a well-documented tendency to overprice longshots all push it around, most of all at the extremes.

Expert ratings

The oldest tradition in the business, and the one that refuses to give you a number. A rating is an ordered category — Safe, Likely, Lean, Toss-up — produced by people who talk to campaigns.

We collect ratings from Cook Political Report, Sabato's Crystal Ball, Inside Elections, Decision Desk HQ and Fox News' power rankings, transcribed from Wikipedia's ratings tables under CC BY-SA 4.0 with attribution.

They are kept out of every average on purpose. Turning “Lean R” into a margin requires picking a number, and whichever number you pick is a modelling assumption doing real work while looking like data entry. What ratings are good for is disagreement: when four raters look at the same seat and two say Toss-up and two say Likely R, that spread is information, and it is shown as a spread on the comparisons page rather than averaged into silence.

What is not published, and why

Three separate restrictions, none of them the same thing.

Sources that permit collection but not republication

Several forecasters allow us to read and store their numbers but not to reprint them race by race during the cycle. Those appear only inside an average with at least three gated contributors, so no individual forecast can be backed out by subtraction. The floor counts gated sources rather than all sources for exactly that reason: a source we publish by name adds nothing to anyone's protection, because a reader can subtract it straight back out. That rule is enforced in code, and the aggregator re-derives it from its own output and refuses to write if it is violated. {{- if gt $d.suppressed_cells 0 }} Today that withholds {{ $d.suppressed_cells }} cells. {{- end }}

Licensed indices, and why there is no House table

The House model exists and runs daily. It is not published, and the reason is arithmetic rather than caution. District forecasts are built from a licensed partisan index, and given the national tide — which we do publish — that index is exactly recoverable from any district forecast:

PVI = (district margin − national tide) ÷ 2

So publishing 435 district margins would republish the index itself. The district run writes to a gitignored directory and stays there. This becomes publishable the day we hold a district-level presidential baseline we are allowed to redistribute, which is a licensing question rather than a modelling one. The Senate table has no such problem: its state lean is our own reconstruction from public-domain official returns, so it is ours to publish.

What is published

Category averages, our own models in full, the individual forecasts whose licences permit it, and a manifest of SHA-256 hashes for every capture, committed on the day it was taken. The hashes are a commitment without a disclosure: when the full archive is released after the election, anyone can verify that the released bytes are bit-identical to what was collected on the dates claimed. Same logic as preregistration.
